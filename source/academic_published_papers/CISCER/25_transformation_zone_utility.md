# Clinical Utility of Cytological Methylation Assay in Cervical Cancer Screening Across Various Cervical Transformation Zones

## ABSTRACT

Type I to III cervical transformation zones (TZ-1 to TZ-3) may have various effects on cervical cancer screening and diagnostic outcomes. This study aims to evaluate the clinical utility of the cytological PAX1/JAM3 methylation (CISCER) assay in cervical cancer screening, especially in TZ-3 women. Between November 2020 and October 2022, 1782 women referred for colposcopy underwent liquid-based cytology (LBC), high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) genotyping, and CISCER testing. After applying inclusion criteria, 1398 women were analyzed, including 1190 with documented TZ classification. Diagnostic performance for detecting cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 or worse (CIN3+) was compared across TZ subgroups. For CIN3+ detection, CISCER showed sensitivities of 67.4%, 72.0%, 48.3%, and 81.5% and specificities of 91.0%, 94.2%, 91.3%, and 89.8% in all women, TZ-1, TZ-2, and TZ-3, respectively. Compared with LBC ≥ ASC-US, hrHPV positivity, or HPV16/18 positivity, CISCER achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy, with AUC values of 0.792, 0.831, 0.698, and 0.856 across the respective groups. In both the overall cohort and TZ-3 subgroup, CISCER positivity was associated with the highest CIN3+ risk (34.6% and 31.4%) and required the fewest colposcopy referrals per CIN3+ detected (2.9 and 3.2). Combining hrHPV testing with CISCER further improved risk stratification. CISCER demonstrated superior diagnostic performance across TZ types, particularly in TZ-3, enabling improved CIN3+ risk discrimination while substantially reducing unnecessary colposcopy referrals compared with conventional cytology and HPV genotyping. 

## What's New?

The anatomical transformation zones of the cervix are critical sites for the development of cervical cancer. The colposcopic evaluation of TZ-3, in particular, is often challenging. Here, the authors sought to determine the optimal screening approach for the different transformation zones. They found that cytological PAX1/JAM3 methylation (CISCER) testing provides superior diagnostic performance across all cervical transformation zones, particularly in TZ-3, compared with traditional cytology and HPV genotyping. CISCER not only improves risk stratification for CIN3+ lesions but also reduces unnecessary colposcopy referrals. The new approach may offer a more accurate and efficient screening method in cervical cancer prevention programs. 

## 1 | Introduction

Cervical cancer, mainly caused by high-risk (HR) human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, is one of the most common malignant tumors in women and is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death in women worldwide [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) 90–70–90 targets (90% of fully vaccinated girls by the age of 15 years, 70% of females undergoing cervical cancer screening by high-performance test at 35 and 45 years of age, and treatment for 90% of women identi fied with cervical disease) are designed to prevent more than 62 million cervical cancer-related deaths by the year 2120 [2]. Currently, the commonly used screening methods for cervical cancer include HPV and cytological testing [3]. HPV testing offers high sensitivity and strong negative predictive value, making it highly effective in detecting cervical cancer and high-grade lesions [4, 5]. However, its low specificity often leads to over-diagnosis, resulting in unnecessary follow-up procedures for transient infections [6]. On the other hand, liquid-based cytology (LBC) enhances the quality of cytological samples by reducing contamination and allowing for multiple tests from the same sample, improving diagnostic accuracy [7]. Nevertheless, LBC's moderate sensitivity and specificity, coupled with its reliance on trained cytologists and specialized infrastructure, limit its widespread use, particularly in resource-limited settings [8]. 

The transformation zones (TZs) of the cervix, located between the original and new squamocolumnar junctions (SCJs), are critical sites for the development of cervical cancer and its precursors, such as grade 2 cervical intraepithelial neoplasia or severe lesions (CIN2+). Studies indicate that nearly all high-grade cervical lesions occur within the TZs [9]. A comprehensive understanding of TZs is essential for assessing lesion progression, evaluating cervical cancer risk, and formulating diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [10]. However, the examination of transformation zone 3 (TZ-3) presents particular challenges [11]. As TZ-3 resides within the endocervical canal, colposcopic evaluation is often inadequate, leading to poor visibility, misidentification, and underdiagnosis [12]. 

DNA methylation of tumor suppressor genes in promoter regions is a hallmark of progressive oncogenesis [13, 14]. 

Methylation levels of specific tumor suppressor genes in the cervix increase with the severity of CIN grade, peaking in cervical cancer [15, 16]. DNA methylation testing has been increasingly proven as a promising cervical screening method owing to its good performance and reproducibility [17]. Several studies have also demonstrated the role of the PAX1/JAM3 methylation test in cervical cancer screening, particularly in the triage of HR HPV-positive women [18, 19] or triage of abnormal cytological results [20]. Although methylation testing has been used in clinical cervical cancer screening for many years, existing literature primarily focuses on its role as a triage method, with varying sensitivities and specificities. Currently, there is no data on the effectiveness of methylation testing across different TZ types. 

This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of different cervical cancer detection methods—specifically, LBC, HPV DNA testing, and PAX1/JAM3 methylation (CISCER)—across different cervical TZs. By comparing the diagnostic performance of these methods, we seek to determine the optimal screening approach tailored to the unique characteristics of different TZs. 

## 2 | Materials and Methods

## 2.1    |    Participants and Study Design

Participants were enrolled from the multicenter two cohorts, that is, METHY3 and METHY4 studies, conducted between November 2020 and October 2022 (NCT04646954 and NCT05290428 registered at clinicaltrials.gov). All participants underwent colposcopy at the study center and collected hrHPV genotyping, LBC testing, and PAX1<sup>m</sup>/JAM3<sup>m</sup> testing results. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 18 years of age or older; intact cervix; no serious immunodeficiency conditions of HIV infection; history of organ transplantation; treatment with immunosuppressive drugs; and full consent. Conversely, we excluded women who had known malignant tumors of the female genital tract or who had active malignant tumors in other sites that were still under treatment. 

## 2.2    |    Sample Collection, Cytology Testing and hrHPV Genotyping

All eligible participants underwent gynecological examinations and cervical cytology sampling. The cytology samples were sent for hrHPV and LBC assays, and the residual samples were sent for PAX1/JAM3 methylation assays. 

All the samples were subjected to high-performance HPV testing on a Cobas 4800 system (Roche Molecular Systems Inc., MN, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions [21]. The Cobas HPV test is an in vitro quantitative technique for detecting HR HPV DNA via polymerase chain reaction amplification that can detect HPV16, HPV18, and other HPV12 types (31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68). 

The LBC and imaging system manual was used for cytology tests (Hologic Inc., MA, USA). Cytologists used the 2014 Bethesda system [22] to classify the cytology results, which include categories such as negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM), atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC-US), low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), atypical squamous cells that cannot exclude highgrade lesion (ASC-H), high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL), atypical glandular cells (AGC), adenocarcinoma (ADC), and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). 

## 2.3    |    DNA Methylation Assays

Methylation was detected in a certified DNA laboratory, and the operators and staff members were blinded to the patients clinical information, LBC test results, HPV genotyping results, and cervical histopathology results. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from the exfoliated cervical sample by using the JH-DNA Isolation and Purification Kit (OriginPoly Bio-Tec Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The DNA concentration was quantified via a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, DE, USA). The PAX1<sup>m</sup> and JAM3<sup>m</sup> levels were determined via the Human PAX1 and JAM3 Gene Methylation Detection Kit (real-time PCR) for Cervical Cancer [Class III medical devices approved by the National Medical Products Administration (No. 20233400253)] with glyceraldehyde-3- phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal control (OriginPoly Bio-Tec Co. Ltd., Beijing, China). The hypermethylation level of the PAX1 and JAM3 genes was determined by the difference between the two Ct values (ΔCt PAX1 = Ct PAX1-Ct GAPDH and ΔCt JAM3 = Ct JAM3-Ct GAPDH). In accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, a positive CISCER (PAX1<sup>m</sup>/JAM3<sup>m</sup>) test result was defined as ΔCt PAX1 ≤ 6.6 or ΔCt JAM3 ≤ 10.0. 

## 2.4 | Colposcopy and Histology Evaluation

All enrolled patients underwent colposcopy performed by the investigators at the hospital, and the standardized colposcopic examination of the cervix was performed by qualified gynecologists. Each examination was performed with 3%–5% acetic acid and Schiller test. Lesions were described in terms of color, margin, vessels, and iodine-staining characteristics. Specimens for colposcopy-directed biopsies were obtained at targeted areas with acetowhitening metaplasia or higher abnormalities from distinct acetowhite lesions on the Schiller test. 

The cervical TZ type was determined by the colposcopist according to the classification of TZ outlined by the International Federation for Cervical Pathology and Colposcopy [23]. There are three types of TZ: TZ type I is completely ectocervical and fully visible; TZ type II has a fully visible endocervical component and may have an ectocervical component that may be small or large; and TZ type III has an endocervical component that is not fully visible and may have an ectocervical component that may be small or large. Cervical biopsies were obtained at suspicious lesion areas, or random biopsies were performed in the four quadrants of the cervix if no suspected lesions were found. ECC was recommended for those with a cervix with TZ type III [24]. Finally, pathological investigations were performed by one pathologist. Pathological diagnosis according to the CIN reporting system was used as the gold standard [25]. 

## 2.5 | Statistical Analysis

SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version 4.1.2, Vienna, Austria) were used for all the statistical analyses. The participants were characterized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables are presented as numbers with percentages, and continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3) or means with standard deviations (mean ± SD). The ΔCt values of methylated PAX1 and JAM3 were compared between groups via one-way ANOVA. The measurement data are shown as %, while comparisons between groups were analyzed by the chi-square test. Logistic regression models were used to calculate odds ratios (ORs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to analyze the factors related to cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 or worse (CIN3+). In the overall analysis of this study, all enrolled participants were included, including women with an unknown TZ status. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn to evaluate the areas under the curve (AUC) for CIN3+ detection. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value for detecting CIN3+ lesions were calculated with 95% CIs. The sensitivity and specificity for detecting CIN3+ lesions were compared via McNemar's test. All tests were two-sided, and differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 for 95% CIs. 

## 3 | Results

## 3.1 | Basic Characteristics of Patients

As shown in Figure 1, a total of 1398 women who underwent colposcopic examination for cervical cancer were included in this study. The median age of the participants was 42years [range: 35–54]. Of these, 466 (33.3%) were postmenopausal, and 932 (66.7%) were premenopausal (Table  S1). TZ-1, TZ-2, and TZ-3 accounted for 20.2% (283/1398), 29.3% (409/1398), and 35.9% (498/1398), respectively. Among them, 293 postmenopausal women were classified as TZ-3. Additionally, 14.9% of patients could not be classified into specific TZs due to incomplete records. Among the study population, 857 women (61.3%) had normal findings or inflammation, 350 (25.0%) had CIN1, 99 (7.1%) had CIN2, 85 (6.1%) had CIN3, and 7 (0.5%) were diagnosed with cervical cancer, including 6 cases of SCC and 1 case of ADC. Colposcopic impressions revealed that 554 women (41.0%) had cervical lesions, and 45 women (3.3%) had vaginal wall lesions. Figure S1 illustrates the discrepancies between colposcopic impressions and histopathological diagnoses, with varying differences observed across different TZ types.

## 3.4 | Performance of Different Methods for CIN3+ Detection in All Women and Subgroups by TZ Types

Table 2 presents the diagnostic performance of various CIN3+ detection methods, including AUC, sensitivity, specificity, 

PPV, NPV, and ORs for CIN3+, categorized by cervical TZ type. Among all participants, CISCER testing showed the highest diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.792 (0.743–0.841) (Figure 3A), sensitivity of 67.4% (57.8–77.0), and specificity of 91% (89.5–92.6). Single-gene tests demonstrated similar effectiveness, with PAX1 showing an AUC of 0.754 (0.702–0.805) and JAM3 an AUC of 0.76 (0.709–0.811). hrHPV(+) had a relatively low AUC of 0.512 (0.5–0.524) but extremely high sensitivity of 98.9% (96.8–100), very low specificity of 3.5% (2.5–4.5), and a PPV of 6.7% (5.4–8.1). Cytology tests also showed limited diagnostic performance, with LBC ≥ ASC-US having an AUC of 0.531 (0.506–0.555) and LBC ≥ LSIL an AUC of 0.672 (0.623–0.721). The CISCER assay demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity, resulting in exceptional diagnostic performance. Its OR was approximately 10 times higher than that of hrHPV+ or LBC ≥ ASC-US, highlighting its superior discriminatory capability. Additionally, it exhibited a lower colposcopy referral rate. 

Figure 3B–D display the diagnostic performance of different testing methods across the different TZs (TZ-1, TZ-2, and TZ-3) groups. It can be observed that CISCER demonstrates the best diagnostic performance across all TZs, with AUC values of 0.831 (0.74–0.922) in TZ-1, 0.698 (0.604–0.792) in TZ-2, and 0.856 (0.781–0.932) in TZ-3. The single-gene test JAM3 shows a relatively high AUC of 0.859 (0.779–0.94) in TZ-3, though the difference compared to CISCER is not statistically significant (p > 0.05). For CIN3+ detection, hrHPV consistently exhibits the highest sensitivity across all zones, with values of 100 (100–100) in TZ-1, 96.6 (80.4–99.8) in TZ-2, and 100 (100–100) in TZ-3. The highest specificity is observed with JAM3, achieving 97.7 (95.8–99.5) in TZ-1 and 94.1 (91.9–96.2) in TZ-3; the specificity of PAX1 is highest in TZ-2, reaching 94.5 (92.2–96.8). 

## 3.5 | Absolute CIN3+ Risks of Different Diagnostic Methods

The study evaluated the absolute risk of CIN3+ and the number of referrals needed to detect one CIN3+ case across various diagnostic methods and stratifications (Table 3). A positive CISCER test resulted in the highest absolute CIN3+ risk (34.6%, 27.7%– 42.1%), far exceeding the 12.4% (9.7%–15.6%) absolute CIN3+ risk with LBC ≥ LSIL and the 10.4% (7.9%–13.4%) absolute CIN3+ risk for HPV16/18+. Combining hrHPV and CISCER further stratified risk; hrHPV or CISCER+ cases showed a 6.7% risk (14.9 referrals), while hrHPV and CISCER+ cases had a 34.8% (27.9%–42.3%) risk, requiring 2.9 referrals. These findings highlight the effectiveness of DNA methylation markers in triaging CIN3+ risk with fewer referrals compared to traditional methods.

## 4 | Discussion

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of various methods, including DNA methylation markers (PAX1 and JAM3), cytology, and HPV genotyping, for the detection of CIN3+ lesions. Our findings demonstrate that CISCER testing provides superior diagnostic performance across all cervical TZs, particularly in TZ-3, where it exhibited high sensitivity and specificity. Furthermore, DNA methylation markers, such as PAX1 and JAM3, showed a significantly higher predictive value for CIN3+ compared to traditional methods, with a notable reduction in the number of referrals required. These results highlight the potential of integrating DNA methylation markers into cervical cancer screening programs, offering a more efficient and accurate approach to triaging HR lesions.

Similarly, in the present study, 14.9% of patients undergoing colposcopy could not be accurately classified into a specific TZ. Compared to previous studies, our study seems to have a higher proportion of TZ-3. Qiao et al. [29] reported that the distribution of TZ1, TZ-2, and TZ-3 was approximately even, with around 32% for TZ1, 38% for TZ2, and 30% for TZ-3. However, in our study, TZ-3 accounted for 35.6%, which is higher than the previously reported proportion. This study found that the age of patients in TZ-3 (53, IQR: 42–59) was significantly older than those in TZ-2 (38, IQR: 33–45) and TZ-1 (35, IQR: 31–39). Furthermore, Fan et al. [30] investigated 2262 cases and found that TZ-1 was more prevalent in women under 30, while TZ-3 was more common in women over 50, a finding consistent with our study. 

We evaluated the relationship between LBC results, high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) types, CISCER testing, and cervical pathology. The results showed that, unlike the generally higher positivity rates of LBC ≥ ASC-US and hrHPV across all pathological groups, CISCER testing exhibited lower positivity rates in the normal and CIN1 groups, but significantly increased in the CIN2 and higher groups. These findings suggest that CISCER testing has higher sensitivity for identifying more severe cervical lesions and may become an important supplementary tool in cervical cancer screening. Numerous studies have evaluated the performance of methylation markers like PAX1 and JAM3 in cervical cancer screening or triage. For instance, in hrHPV-positive women, the sensitivity of CISCER tests for detecting CIN2+ lesions was 92.6% (95% CI: 87.2%– 97.9%), with a specificity of 95.7% (95% CI: 93.4%–98%) [20]. For non-16/18-hrHPV-positive women, the sensitivity and specificity of CISCER tests for detecting CIN3+ lesions were 84.8% and 88.5%, respectively [19]. For women undergoing triage of abnormal cytological results, the CISCER test showed a sensitivity of 83.8% and a specificity of 95.8% for detecting CIN2+ [31]. Additionally, this study compared the diagnostic performance of different tests across various TZs. CISCER demonstrated superior diagnostic performance across all TZs, especially in TZ-3, where its diagnostic performance for CIN3+ reached an AUC of 0.856 (95% CI: 0.781–0.932). This may be attributed to the correlation between DNA methylation and age, as methylation levels tend to increase with age [32]. Based on the above analysis, we believe that DNA methylation can serve as an effective cervical cancer detection method for women with TZ-3. 

We found that although CISCER exhibited the optimal diagnostic performance across all TZ types, the AUC value of TZ-2 was slightly lower than those of TZ-1 and TZ-3. We speculate that this discrepancy may stem from the unique biological and clinical characteristics of the TZ-2 population, rather than inherent limitations of the detection method itself. In this study, TZ-2 women in our cohort were significantly younger (median age 38years, IQR: 33–45) compared to TZ-3 women (median age 53years, IQR: 42–59). Given that DNA methylation levels are known to accumulate progressively with age and lesion severity [32], th younger age profile of TZ-2 women may partly account for the observed difference in AUC. In addition, TZ-2 encompasses both ectocervical and fully visible endocervical components, which may give rise to a more heterogeneous distribution of lesions across varying stages of maturity, potentially introducing greater variability in methylation signals. Nonetheless, even in this population, CISCER retained the highest diagnostic performance among all methods evaluated, underscoring its consistent superiority across all TZ types. 

We analyzed the immediate risk of CIN3+ based on different test results and found that the OR for CISCER+ was approximately 10 times higher than that for hrHPV+ or LBC ≥ ASC-US, highlighting its superior discriminatory capability. Its absolute CIN3+ risk was also much higher than that of hrHPV+ or LBC ≥ ASC-US (34.6% [27.7%–42.1%], 6.7% [5.5%–8.2%], and 7.0% [5.6%–8.6%], respectively). In contrast, the absolute risk for CISCER− was only 2.5% (1.7%–3.5%). This enhanced discriminatory capability allows for more accurate risk stratification, meaning that CISCER could be used to prioritize referrals for colposcopy in women with higher risk, thereby optimizing resource utilization and reducing unnecessary procedures for women with lower risk. A similar trend was observed among TZ-3 women, where the absolute risk for CISCER+ was 31.4%, while the absolute risk for CISCER− was only 1.2%. This enhanced discriminatory capability further supports the utility of CISCER in improving risk stratification for TZ-3 women. 

This study demonstrates that CISCER, as a method for cervical cancer screening, is highly effective for detecting CIN3+, outperforming traditional cervical cancer screening methods such as cytology and HPV DNA genotyping. Notably, among TZ-3 women, CISCER exhibited higher accuracy and superior risk stratification capability compared to conventional methods. Several limitations of the present study warrant consideration. First, this study population comprised women who had already been referred for colposcopy, representing a higher-risk group than the general screening population. This enriched disease prevalence is substantially higher than that typically observed in unselected screening populations, which may influence the absolute values of PPV and limit the direct generalizability of the diagnostic estimates to a primary screening context. Furthermore, as histological confirmation was only available for women who underwent colposcopy, and given that methylation levels are known to correlate with lesion severity, the performance metrics reported here should be interpreted primarily in the context of triage for HR women, such as those who are hrHPV-positive or have abnormal cytology rather than as direct estimates for population-based primary screening. These considerations do not diminish the clinical value of CISCER; rather, they highlight the importance of future prospective, population-based cohort studies to further characterize its performance across the full spectrum of disease severity. Finally, lesion size was not systematically recorded in the study protocols; prospective collection of this variable in future studies would allow further exploration of its potential relationship with methylation levels and diagnostic performance overall diagnostic performance. 

## 5 | Conclusions

This study demonstrates that CISCER testing has high diagnostic performance in cervical cancer screening. As an emerging screening method, it has the potential to become an important tool for early cervical cancer detection.
