In the gynecology clinic waiting area, you can often overhear conversations like: "Doctor, my period hasn't stopped for half a month — could it be cancer?" "I've been postmenopausal for three years and suddenly started bleeding again — is something seriously wrong with my uterus?" Abnormal uterine bleeding is one of the most common presenting complaints among women, and the most panic-inducing aspect is equating "bleeding" directly with "endometrial cancer." In reality, uterine bleeding is a "warning signal" from the body, but behind this signal may lie multiple causes — endometrial cancer is only one of them, not the whole story.

**I. What Is Abnormal Uterine Bleeding?**

Simply put, it's "irregular menstruation": periods that don't come when expected, bleeding when it shouldn't occur, periods lasting more than 7 days, or bleeding so heavy that you soak through a sanitary pad every hour (even leading to anemia).

**II. The "Hidden Culprits" Behind Bleeding: 90% Are Benign**

Endometrial cancer may present with bleeding as a first symptom (especially in postmenopausal women), but over 90% of abnormal bleeding is benign. Common causes include:

1. Hormonal imbalance: Pubertal/perimenopausal ovarian dysfunction (most common);
2. Structural uterine issues: Uterine fibroids (submucosal type), endometrial polyps, adenomyosis;
3. Others: Pelvic inflammatory disease, coagulation disorders, etc.
4. High-risk alert: Only about 1%–10% of bleeding is related to endometrial cancer, and it is most common in women over 50 who are obese, diabetic, chronically anovulatory (e.g., PCOS), or have a family history.

**III. What Bleeding Signals Should Raise Concern?**

If you experience any of the following, seek medical evaluation promptly:

* Postmenopausal bleeding (even just a spot on underwear);
* Bleeding lasting >7 days, or repeated "periods" within one month;
* Heavy bleeding (e.g., soaking a pad every hour) accompanied by dizziness or fatigue;
* Foul-smelling discharge, sudden weight loss, or decreased appetite.

**IV. What to Expect at the Doctor: Pathology Is the "Gold Standard"**

Physicians will determine the cause through the following steps:

* Initial evaluation: Gynecologic ultrasound (assessing endometrial thickness, uterine/ovarian structure), complete blood count;
* Key diagnostic procedure: Hysteroscopy + D&C (directly visualizing the endometrium and obtaining tissue for pathology — the pathology result determines the nature);
* Supplementary tests: Sex hormones (assessing ovarian function), tumor markers (auxiliary assessment).

**VI. Even If It's Not Cancer, Don't Delay!**

Long-term abnormal bleeding may lead to anemia, infection, or mask other conditions (such as growing fibroids or polyp malignant transformation). Benign lesions can be managed with medication (hormone regulation) or surgery (hysteroscopic polyp/fibroid removal). The 5-year survival rate for early-stage (Stage I) endometrial cancer exceeds 90% — early detection is key.

**Summary:**
