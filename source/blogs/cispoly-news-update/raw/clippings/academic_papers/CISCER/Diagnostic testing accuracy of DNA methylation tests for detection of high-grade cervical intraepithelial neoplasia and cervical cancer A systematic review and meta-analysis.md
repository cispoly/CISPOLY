## 1\. Background

Cervical screening detects high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), which can then be treated to avoid progression to invasive cervical cancer (ICC). Recently, many countries have implemented primary high-risk Human papillomavirus (hrHPV) testing, which can detect CIN2 or worse (CIN2+) with higher sensitivity when compared to cytology-based screening, at nearly 90%,. However, 13% of all women will test positive for hrHPV in each screening round, resulting in a low specificity for detection of CIN2+. Triage tests are therefore used to select women that should be referred to colposcopy. Cytology is the most commonly used triage test with existing infrastructure, although it has several limitations. Perhaps most importantly, cytology-based triage does not perform well against long-term cancer risk. A randomised policy trial found that the risk of ICC was 1.3 (95%CI 0·6–2·4) per 100,000 person-years in women who were hrHPV negative, and 318.2 per 100,000 person-years in those HPV16/18 positive and cytology negative, which highlights the lack of protection when cytology triage is relied upon. Cytology sensitivity for CIN2+ is modest, estimated at between 53% and 75%,, and there is significant inter- and intra-observer variability in cytological diagnoses. Availability of trained cytopathologists is limited, particularly in countries where the burden of cervical cancer remains high. With the introduction of hrHPV self-sampling as a primary screening method in some countries, with similar accuracy to physician-taken samples, triage with cytology necessitates a further clinician-collected sample.

DNA methylation is a chemical, measurable, epigenetic change, that forms part of a regulatory process influencing gene expression. Methylation describes the addition of a methyl group to specific cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) sites on the DNA. The relationship between methylation and gene expression is nuanced, but typically hypermethylation of a promoter region results in reduced expression. Methylation becomes aberrant in cancerous cells, which is shown to correlate with carcinogenic transformation.

Methylation at different CpG sites in both human and HPV viral genomes, in one particular gene, panels or indices (such as the WID-CIN ), have been proposed as diagnostic tests for ICC, CIN3+, or CIN2+,,, *.* Some of these tests have shown similar or improved sensitivity and/or specificity compared to other triage methods,. Multiple groups worldwide have individually identified and assessed different genes and gene panels, which are all separate tests, and few head-to-head comparisons exist. As hrHPV testing has been implemented in screening programmes internationally, clinically meaningful information on the diagnostic test accuracy of methylation markers for triage needs to be assessed in hrHPV positive populations.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to collate and critically appraise the evidence on the diagnostic accuracy of all published human and viral DNA methylation tests for cervical precancer and cancer. We explored their accuracy, specifically in hrHPV positive populations as the most relevant clinical indication but also in broader applications as a primary screening test.

## 2\. Methods

### 2.1. Search strategy and selection criteria

We registered the study in PROSPERO (CRD42022299760), and published a protocol for the study in an open-access journal. Our review was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for diagnostic test accuracy study guidelines,. The population, index test, and target condition approach was used to define study eligibility as recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Diagnostic Test Accuracy. ()

We included randomised, cross-sectional, prospective and retrospective cohort studies reporting on any population of adult human women, including biobank-based studies. We excluded studies where the number of cases (e.g. CIN2+ cases) was less than five. All DNA methylation tests (“markers”) were considered eligible, applied using any methylation technology, to any gynaecological sample containing DNA material from cervical cells ().

Where authors defined an index (methylation) test as ‘positive’ or ‘negative’, we accepted and used these to define true and false positive and negative results in comparison to the reference standards. Where studies presented multiple ‘cut-offs’, we extracted data for the cut-off that yielded the most overall accurate results, but this would lead to the study being marked down in the risk of bias due to a lack of pre-defined cut-off.

We included only studies using histology as a reference standard; with the exception of patients with negative hrHPV tests and/or normal cytology and/or normal colposcopy, where a histological biopsy would not be indicated clinically (in accordance with colposcopy guidelines in Europe,, ). We accepted papers reporting a diagnosis of low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) or high-grade squamous intra-epithelial lesion (HSIL) as equivalent to CIN1 and CIN2–3, respectively. Given the heterogeneity of CIN2 and that half of these lesions regress spontaneously, our primary objective was to determine the best test for diagnosis of CIN3+ lesions in hrHPV positive populations, and secondary objectives included both CIN2+ and ICC as endpoints. We focused on methylation for primary diagnostics and did not include studies using methylation in a post-treatment or ‘test of cure’ setting.

MEDLINE, Embase and Cochrane CENTRAL were searched from inception until 31st March 2025 with no language restriction (full search strategy listed in supplement). For included studies, study characteristics were recorded. Where possible, raw data were extracted including true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false negatives (FN) and false positives (FP). Otherwise these parameters were calculated, which required both reported accuracy (for example sensitivity and specificity) and the number of diseased and non-diseased subjects.

Risk of bias (ROB) and applicability was assessed independently by two authors (LBE and KSK) with the QUADAS−2 tool, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

### 2.2. Data analysis

Each gene or gene panel was analysed separately. Throughout the manuscript we use the inclusive term “methylation marker”, referring to the following: established commercially-available assays (e.g. S5, Gyntect), and specific genes (e.g. HPV16 *L1*), or sometimes a panel of genes (e.g. *PAX1/SOX1*), without connection to a commercially-available assay. Our primary analysis focused on hrHPV positive women only, with a secondary analysis including all women, irrespective of hrHPV status.

For each specified test (individual gene or gene panel; “marker”) with four or more studies eligible for inclusion, we conducted bivariate random-effects meta-analyses to estimate sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), positive likelihood ratio (LR+), and negative likelihood ratio (LR-) (which are not influenced by disease prevalence ) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We produced Summary Receiver Operating Characteristic curves, and forest plots for each marker. The hierarchical SROC (HSROC) model by Rutter and Gatsonis, is mathematically equivalent to the bivariate model by Reitsma et *al*. when no covariates are included,.

For the hrHPV positive CIN3+ analysis, we produced pre-test post-test probability plots, using the ppp procedure in STATA,. The baseline pre-test probability of CIN3+ among women testing hrHPV test positive in low (5%), moderate (9%), and high (17%) risk settings have been reported recently. Decision thresholds for referral to colposcopy were identified from benchmark risk levels applied in Europe, where post-test probabilities ≥ 10% were defined as sufficiently high-risk for colposcopy referral, from 1% to 10% as moderate-risk, and < 1% as low-risk.

Analyses were performed in the statistical programme R version 4.4.2 using the *lme4* package, as per the Cochrane DTA Handbook. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) tool was used to assess the certainty of the evidence,.

In addition to examining heterogeneity, publication bias was assessed using the method by Deeks *et al.*,. Given the limited statistical power of this test, a p-value less than 0.10 was considered indicating evidence of publication bias,, ()

We performed sensitivity analyses including only studies with a low risk of bias. We performed a second sensitivity analysis specifically excluding case-control studies, which can lead to overestimation of diagnostic accuracy. Similarly, as there is potential for methylation levels to be affected by the platform used, we conducted a separate analysis stratified by the type of quantitative methylation testing technique. ()

## 3\. Results

We identified 2097 studies after removal of duplicates, 784 of which were eligible for full-text review. After review, 125 studies with 49,242 women were included in the systematic review ().

### 3.1. Study characteristics

The included studies () were mostly cohort studies (81/125, 64.8%), case-control studies (44/125, 35.2%) and few biobank-based studies (7/125, 6.5%) examining methylation in archived specimens from diseased and non-diseased subjects. There were no randomised trials. We retrieved 51 studies (51/125, 40.8%) where results were reported either totally or separately in HPV positive women. Of these, 39 (31.2%, 39/125) studies reported for hrHPV positive cohorts (12–14 recognised high-risk types \[without genotyping\], and 12 studies (reporting on HPV16 *L1* methylation) reported for HPV type 16 positive cohorts only (9.6%, 12/125). The most common reported population after ‘hrHPV positive’ was ‘colposcopy clinic’ (18.4%, 23/125), followed by ‘abnormal cytology’ (12%, 15/125). There were insufficient granular data to examine the impact of study covariates (such as age, technology, or sample type separately). However, in all studies reporting data on HPV positive women, only cervical smear (brush/scrape) samples were used. (Supplementary Results).

### 3.2. Risk of bias and applicability

Only one study was assessed as having a low ROB in all four domains with the QUADAS−2 assessment tool (1/125, 0.8%) (). In the patient selection domain, 54 studies were judged as high-risk of bias (54/125, 43.2%), due to a case-control design or exclusions judged to be inappropriate. The minority of the studies (21/125, 16.8%) specified that the methylation (index) test was conducted blinded to the results of the reference standard. Additionally, over half of the studies (66/125, 52.8%) were not clear whether the reference standard was conducted without knowledge of the index test; however, this was inferred for case-control studies where the histology status was known a priori. Applicability concerns were judged to be low across all three evaluated domains in 21 studies (21/125, 16.8%) where the included population, conducted nature of the index test and reference standard were felt to be applicable to the use of the test in a relevant clinical setting.

## 4\. Discussion

A number of markers displayed good accuracy for CIN3+ detection in hrHPV positive women. When ranked by pooled DOR, *JAM3, C13ORF18*, and *PAX1* were the top performers; however, high heterogeneity resulted in wide confidence intervals, as commonly observed in diagnostic test accuracy meta-analyses. When *JAM3* or *PAX1* are applied in a pre-test post-test probability model, assuming a 5% risk of CIN3+ in hrHPV positive women, the risk of CIN3+ increased to above 20%, easily surpassing the 10% threshold for high-risk referral to colposcopy, if methylation positive, and decreased under 2% if methylation negative, falling within the moderate-risk of referral threshold according to benchmark risk levels applied in Europe and the US. The lowest post-test probability was *EPB41L3* at 1.35%, which would be an exceptionally low result to be achieved within a single screening round, and is lower than current estimates for cytology.

Overall, the quality of the studies was low to moderate; 108/125 (86.4%) had less than three of four domains classified as low risk of bias. However, after sensitivity analysis excluding studies with a high risk of bias, the majority of results were largely unchanged. In fact, in hrHPV positive women six markers at CIN3+ (6/12, 50%) and six markers at CIN2+ (6/11, 54.5%) actually demonstrated an increase in diagnostic accuracy when only studies with a low risk of bias were used, suggesting that here, bias did not overinflate the accuracy. Similarly, after stratifying our analysis by quantitative methylation testing assay showed largely consistent results. This may indicate that variations in methylation testing methodology are unlikely to be a major source of heterogeneity, supporting the generalisability of our findings across testing platforms. The majority of studies used QMSP and this continues to be the most widely-used platform, representing a good balance of cost and accuracy.

*JAM3*, *C13ORF18* and *PAX1* have been identified as tumour suppressor genes,. Increased expression of *JAM3* has been linked to improved prognosis in breast and colorectal cancer,. *C13ORF18* methylation likely influences carcinogenesis through the suppression of cellular senescence and apoptotic pathways,,. During normal cellular physiology, *PAX1* has been shown to regulate cellular proliferation,,. Notable genes with high accuracy may warrant further in vitro studies examining carcinogenic processes.

Kelly *et al.* published a review including 43 studies examining seven markers in 2019, reporting that *PAX1* achieved a pooled sensitivity of 68.4% (CI 53.0–80.6) and specificity of 83.9% (CI 74.2–90.4) for detecting CIN3+. However, no analysis was conducted in hrHPV positive women, nor was there a validity assessment using a recognised ROB tool. Salta *et al* published a meta-analysis of methylation markers as a triage test in hrHPV positive women in 2023, including 23 studies, which reported that overall, methylation testing achieved a pooled sensitivity of 67% (CI 63–70%) and specificity of 80% (CI 75–83%). However, all markers were pooled into the same analysis. As we have shown, these methylation markers are all different tests with significant variation in their diagnostic accuracy. Our systematic review with meta-analysis includes 125 studies and 49,242 patients and provides the most comprehensive and clinically-relevant analysis to-date.

Our study has limitations. Only eight studies (8/125, 6.4%) assessed methylation within the context of a screening population. The large majority of studies (114/125, 91.2%) were marked as ‘high’ or ‘unclear’ risk of bias in the first domain regarding the included populations. Around a third (43/125, 34.4%) of the studies specified a methylation cut-off before the analysis was conducted. However, results in a sensitivity analysis specifically excluding case-control studies were broadly consistent or in some cases produced higher accuracy estimates. We observed high heterogeneity, which is common in diagnostic test accuracy meta-analyses and likely reflects variation in cut-offs for both methylation and sensitivity and specificity trade-offs across studies. Hence, some analyses (such as HPV16:*L1*, at the ICC endpoint) yielded wide confidence intervals, indicating imprecision in the summary estimates. These limitations, along with observed risk of bias are also reflected in the GRADE where the certainty of evidence was frequently low. Publication bias assessment identified 10 markers with significant funnel plot asymmetry, most commonly within the hrHPV analysis. Consequently, the pooled diagnostic accuracy for these markers may not be fully representative of their true performance, and these findings should be interpreted with caution.

A number of studies instead determined methylation cut-offs post hoc to optimise diagnostic performance, often by maximising Youden’s index, which may have inflated estimates of sensitivity and specificity, and limit their translation into clinical practice. Future studies should incorporate prespecified methylation thresholds to reduce risk of bias within the index test domain and enable meta-analyses aimed at identifying optimal cut-offs for clinical use as a triage tool. We accepted HPV-negative, or cytology-negative and/or normal colposcopy as absence of disease. There is a chance that this may have introduced a small element of bias into the reported results. However, this approach reflects standard clinical practice, where performing biopsies in such individuals would expose them to unnecessary harm without clear clinical benefit, and might rather lead to an underestimation than inflation of the sensitivity estimates of methylation testing. We presented DOR within the main text of the manuscript as a concise summary measure of global test performance across multiple cut-offs, but the full set of accuracy measures is available within our tables and supplement. While sensitivity and specificity, positive and negative predictive values may be more readily interpretable for clinical decision-making, their comparability across studies is limited in this context. Additionally, while LR+ and LR- are not influenced by disease prevalence, relying on solely these may be problematic in the context of clinical variability. We chose to exclude markers with less than four published studies; although the majority that are excluded by this parameter are likely to be those not performing highly in initial study. We present data for the optimal endpoint, although the included data may have some attributed bias, as various clinical settings may be aiming for different clinical endpoints.

We were unable to perform various planned subgroup analyses due to insufficient data, and therefore in our analysis including ‘all data’ we have combined methylation tests using tissue samples and LBC samples. These estimates may be very similar, probably because they may include DNA from similar cell proportions. Regardless, all studies in our primary analysis used LBC (cervical cell) samples. There were very few studies using self-samples, and no studies with truly long-term (>10 years) data. No studies using non-bisulfite conversion techniques were eligible, but this is a technology worth future exploration. We did not explore the impact of vaccination status as there were no available data to do so. In the current era, we believe that primary diagnostics should not be heavily influenced by vaccination status, as vaccination efficacy has been shown to vary based on age at vaccination. Emerging evidence on differing methylation patterns in high-grade lesions between the vaccinated and unvaccinated exists and vaccination status could therefore be crucial in the future when planning the application of methylation testing in screening and management of HSIL lesions. The impact of patient age, cytology status at baseline, methylation testing technology, collection method, storage and sample material on methylation remains an important area of future research. Based on the currently available evidence, we cannot exclude that these factors are more important than gene or panel selection. In an ideal setting, these would be fully explored as moderating factors in a randomised trial comparing methylation to cytology triage. Currently, business interests add complexity to conducting randomised trials comparing different methylation markers, but this remains crucial to facilitate obtaining robust comparison data. Outside the scope of this work, a comparative meta-analysis involving multiple alternative markers and combinations of markers is needed, as is studies examining self-samples and studies with long-term data.

Compared to clinician-taken samples, CIN2+ detection with hrHPV PCR self-sampling achieves similar accuracy. However, women who test hrHPV positive on a self-sample are usually then recommended to undergo a clinician-collected sample. While women may engage with self-sampling initiatives and this is a viable method to increase uptake, in the absence of a triage pathway suitable for self-samples, those testing hrHPV positive must still navigate the conventional clinical pathway involving clinician testing, thereby limiting the extent to which self-sampling can meaningfully overcome existing barriers to screening. Chang *et al.* did not identify a significant difference in the accuracy of CIN3+ detection using *PAX1* (n = 136, sensitivity 64%, specificity 77% for clinician-collected, and sensitivity 61%, specificity 76% for self-samples). HrHPV testing in urine has also been proposed, with a recent meta-analysis reporting a pooled sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 89%. This is an attractive alternative to patients. Van den Helder *et al.* found *ASCL1/LHX8* methylation in urine had an Area Under the Curve of 0.84 to detect CIN3+. More research is clearly needed to ascertain whether these tests truly work accurately on self-samples or urine.

In the UK between 2023 and 24, 57.2% of all referrals to colposcopy (136,615/238,837) did not undergo a biopsy or excision, suggesting absence of a high-grade lesion. This represents a substantial workload burden, in both cytopathology and colposcopy clinics. DNA methylation testing can be automated, which could prove advantageous in countries with existing screening programmes. One advantage of methylation over genotyping is that methylation provides information on the effect that HPV infection has had on the tissue, rather than simply providing information on the type-specific risk. In low and middle-income countries, barriers to implementation include strained healthcare infrastructure, limited trained cytopathologists, and increased prevalence of cervical neoplasia. Methylation could circumvent these limitations to providing safe, effective, and timely medical intervention and help meet the World Health Organisation’s targets for screening under the condition of low costs,. In these settings, highly sensitive markers such as the S5 classifier may be of even greater value, while a lower specificity may lower its value in highly-screened settings, where more specific markers such as *C13ORF18*, *JAM3* or *PAX1* may be more useful. Although in their current state, many methylation tests remain expensive, the costs of epigenetic platforms are decreasing year on year, and in the future they could help to meet a diagnostic need in both high and low resource settings and ultimately improve care for women globally.

## 5\. Conclusions

DNA methylation markers hold significant promise as diagnostic tests for high-grade CIN, and cervical cancer. We found that in hrHPV positive women, several methylation markers performed well, including some that are not commercial assays, and would be of use in screening either as a primary screening method or as a triage test. Our findings are limited by the quality of the included studies, which was mostly reduced by the selection of the included populations and often a case-control design. For translation of methylation markers into clinical practice, they must be evaluated in high-quality studies of screening cohorts, ideally including on self-samples, and directly compared to other triage methods such as cytology. These studies are currently sparse, and remain crucial for future implementation. Future work should also aim to assess the ability of methylation tests in predicting incident disease, rather than cross-sectional accuracy studies alone.
