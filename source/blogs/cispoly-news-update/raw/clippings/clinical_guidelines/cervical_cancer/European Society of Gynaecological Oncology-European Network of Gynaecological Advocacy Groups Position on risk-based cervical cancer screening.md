# European Society of Gynaecological Oncology/European Network of Gynaecological Advocacy Groups Position on risk-based cervical cancer screening




Elmar Joura<sup>a</sup>, Kimon Chatzistamatiou<sup>b,*</sup> , Murat Gultekin<sup>c</sup> , Jalid Sehouli<sup>d</sup> , Ico Toth<sup>e</sup> , Richard Toth<sup>f</sup> , on behalf of the European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO) 


Received 26 May 2026, Accepted 26 May 2026; Available online 1 June 2026 

## ABSTRACT

The Risk-Based Screening for Cervical Cancer project was a 4-year Horizon 2020 European research consortium that developed and validated a risk stratified approach to cervical cancer screening, moving away from the current one-size-fits-all model. The European Society of Gynecological Oncolog and its network, the European Network of Gynecological Advocacy Groups, participated as partners, contributing their expertise from both professiona medical society and patient advocacy perspectives. 

The project’s key findings support replacing cytology with human papillomavirus (HPV) deoxyribonucleic acid testing as the primary screening modality, as it provides greater protection against invasive cervical cancer. Gender-neutral HPV vaccination is endorsed as a strategy to accelerate herd immunity, particularly where vaccination coverage falls short of the World Health Organization targets. HPV genotyping, alongside age and screening history, emerges as a critical tool for individualizing risk assessment, while methylation tests might be useful tools to significantly reduce unnecessary colposcopy referrals within a fully molecular approach to cervical cancer screening. 

For vaccinated cohorts specifically, the evidence supports longer screening intervals, more conservative management of high-grade lesions caused by non-vaccine HPV types, and the potential substitution of HPV genotyping for standard HPV testing. Self-sampling combined with polymerase chair reaction-based HPV testing is endorsed as a safe, effective strategy to increase participation, particularly among populations who are under-screened with flexible access pathways recommended to address socioeconomic barriers. 

European Society of Gynecological Oncology/European Network of Gynecological Advocacy Groups formally endorse the Risk-Based Screening for Cervica Cancer recommendations and call for the reform of European cervical cancer screening programs through the adoption of risk-stratified, patient-centered strategies, supported by integrated population registries, standardized quality indicators, and continuous real-time monitoring of vaccination and screening outcomes

## BACKGROUND

According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer, cervical cancer is the fourth most common gynecological malignancy worldwide. In 1990, the United Nations Development Program introduced the Human Development Index (HDI), which measures a country’s health, education, and standard of living (https://hdr.undp.org). In countries with a very high HDI, cervical cancer is the sixth most common cancer among females. In countries with a high HDI, it ranks fifth, and in countries with a medium or low HDI, cervical cancer has the second highest agestandardized incidence rate among females (https://gco.iarc.fr/en). This has been largely attributed to inadequate cervical cancer prevention strategies or inadequate access to prevention services.<sup>1</sup> 

Given that cervical cancer is a preventable disease, in August 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) initiated a worldwide campaign to adopt 3 strategic pillars aiming to lead to cervical cancer elimination. Adoption of these strategic pillars globally, by 2030, could reduce cervical cancer incidence to below 4 new cases per 100,000 women per year in the long term, worldwide. The three pillars are: first, achieving a 90% HPV vaccination coverage rate for girls below the age of 15; second, covering 70% of the target population with a highly effective screening test for cervical cancer; and third, offering effective treatment for cervical pre(cancer) to at least 90% of the eligible population.<sup>2</sup> Since 2020, there have been increasing global efforts to advocate for the 3 pillars; however, not all countries start from the same point or have similar resources to take the necessary steps toward cervica cancer elimination.<sup>3</sup> 

There have also been efforts to adapt the WHO pillars for cervical cancer elimination to the specific needs of different regions or countries, acknowledging that a one-size-fits-all approach rarely works and that some fine-tuning is necessary, while still preserving the main idea behind the WHO strategy. This approach is of particular relevance for cervical cancer screening, the second pillar. In recent years, there has been significant research on how to make cervical cancer screening more efficient without compromising accuracy. This rationale was first adopted by the American Society of Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology, when they reported on the risk-based management of abnormal screening results.<sup>4,5</sup> In Europe, the RISCC consortium has further investigated the risk-based approach, examining several parameters of the screening process. 

The aim of this article is to articulate the position of the European Society of Gynecological Oncology (ESGO)/European Network of Gynecological Cancer Advocacy Groups (ENGAGe) regarding the RISCC project and its findings pertaining to cervical cancer screening in Europe. ESGO / ENGAGe joined the RISCC consortium as one of the 11 partners and participated in the Horizon 2020 project for 4 years (2020-2024). ESGO / ENGAGe were primarily responsible for providing the perspectives of a professional medical society and of patients, while also playing a role in the communication and dissemination of the findings of the project.

The development of this statement was conducted under the auspices of ESGO / ENGAGe, following methodologies consistent with the ESGO standard operating procedures. ## RATIONALE

## The RISCC Project: Design and Methods

The acronym RISCC stands for “Risk-based Screening for Cervical Cancer”. The RISCC project was designed and conducted by a consortium of key opinion leaders in HPV-related research in basic science, public health, and clinical science. RISCC was developed in response to the WHO global call for cervical cancer elimination, as well as Europe’s Beating Cancer Plan. The aims of RISCC were to develop and assess a risk-based cervical cancer screening program, to provide widely available implementation tools for an effective cervical cancer prevention strategy, and, thus, to contribute to cervical cancer elimination in Europe (https://www. riscc-h2020.eu/publications/). 

The research topics investigated within the RISCC consortium covered a wide range of cervical cancer prevention questions and implementation strategies. The first topic was the identification of different cervical cancer and pre-cancer risk profiles for women who are unvaccinated and eligible to participate in cervical cancer screening. The database used for this assessment was formed during the CoheaHr project (COmparing HEAlth Services Interventions for the Prevention of HPV-related Cancer) (http://www. coheahr.eu) and included more than 150,000 women. 6-8 

Second, given the expansion of widespread vaccination against HPV, and the subsequent cervical cancer and pre-cancer risk reduction in vaccinated cohorts, the RISCC consortium tried to assess different risk profiles for the development of cervical (pre) cancer, depending on different vaccination coverage rates. 

The third topic investigated within RISCC was the individualized approach of cervical cancer screening, taking into consideration parameters or risk factors which normally would not alter screening within an organized screening program. 

Fourth, RISCC aimed to develop a mathematical model to investigate the differential effects of novel risk-based cervical cancer screening programs on screening outcomes, in particular, on cervical (pre)cancer risk estimation. 

Finally, the RISCC consortium sought to develop mobile and ehealth platforms to approach women eligible for screening and enhance and facilitate their participation in the cervical cancer screening program. Apart from using digitalization as a tool to implement screening, the platforms will also be used to educate healthcare providers and policymakers so that the concept of riskbased screening can be easily adopted and incorporated in different settings. ESGO / ENGAGe were active collaborators to the RISCC consortium pertaining to the latter 2 interventions. 

## The RISCC Project: Outcomes

Following 4 years of rigorous research, the RISCC consortium has reached certain conclusions regarding cervical cancer prevention. 

First, human papillomavirus (HPV) deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) testing should be used as a primary screening test. There is significant research comparing cytology-based cervical cancer screening to HPV-based screening. 

In Europe in particular, there have been large, randomized trials showing that HPV-based screening provides 60% to 70% greater protection against invasive cervical carcinoma compared with cytology.<sup>8</sup> This was largely attributed to the inherent characteristic of higher sensitivity of HPV DNA testing compared to cytology, a fact reported in a Cochrane analysis published in 2017, which also documented the lower specificity of HPV DNA testing; the latter has made the triaging of women with HPV DNA-positive results a necessary step in the pathway to optimal cervical cancer screening.<sup>9</sup> Moreover, it has also been established that, in the case of co-testing, HPV DNA testing confers a higher diagnostic accuracy than cytology.<sup>10</sup> In addition, the WHO recommends screening with HPV DNA testing, including the option of messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) testing, as a primary screening modality in case of physician-only collected cervical samples.<sup>11,12</sup> HPV mRNA testing has statistically similar sensitivity compared to HPV DNA testing and slightly better specificity, characteristics that allow for its usage as a primary HPV screening test, which, as with HPV DNA tests, should be followed by further triage of HPV-positive women to colposcopy.<sup>13</sup> In any case, HPV testing should be based on the use of highly accurate tests that have been clinically validated and adhere to specified quality indicators.<sup>14,15</sup> Therefore, HPV testing is recommended as the primary screening test in organized cervical cancer screening programs, replacing cytology as well as cotesting. 

Second, gender-neutral vaccination accelerates HPV elimination. The first pillar of cervical cancer elimination proposed by the WHO recommends a 90% vaccination coverage rate for girls by the age of 15. However, since the introduction of the HPV vaccine, it has become evident that vaccination of both males and females (gender-neutral vaccination) might be more effective in achieving better herd immunity within vaccinated populations.<sup>16</sup> This would not interfere with the WHO policy as long as cost-effectiveness is taken into consideration. It has been shown that gender-neutral vaccination is cost-effective when vaccination rates are suboptimal; however, once the WHO targets are met, girls-only vaccination achieves better cost-effectiveness.<sup>17</sup> Therefore, if we take into account the challenges of achieving 90% HPV vaccination rates globally, gender-neutral HPV vaccination could be an option to accelerate the cervical cancer elimination efforts.<sup>18</sup> 

Third, HPV genotyping is important in determining cervical cancer risk. In fact, together, with age and cervical cancer screening history, it comprise the main parameters assessed during the RISCC project, which could modify a woman’s risk to have or to acquire cervical pre-cancer. It has been shown that women with a high-risk (hr) HPV infection, detected within primary cervical cancer screening, have an increased risk of being hrHPV positive at a future screening instance even 15 years later, and this risk is even greater in the case of positivity for HPV 16 (odds ratio 3.4 vs 5.8 respectively). Also, the risk for the occurrence of cervical intra-epithelial neoplasia 3 is higher in case of 2 consecutive hrHPV positive results, and particularly for HPV 16, compared to being HPV positive once.<sup>19</sup> Given the fact that the follow-up of detected HPV-positive cases mostly relies on colposcopic evaluation and cytology, the need for further molecular triage is emerging. With the introduction of methylation test into patient pathways, a significant decrease can be achieved in the need for colposcopy referrals. In case of FAM19A4/miR124-2 or ASCL1/ LHX8 methylation tests, the reduction in direct referrals was 86% and 71%, and the reduction in referrals after 1 year was 42% and 36%, respectively. This might be the basis of a more sensitive and automated patient triage pathway.<sup>20</sup> Another parameter that affects cervical cancer screening risk is vaccination against HPV. Vaccination reduces cervical cancer risk, a fact that has been documented in various studies.<sup>21-23</sup> Therefore, vaccinated populations, particularly those with gender-neutral vaccination coverage, might be at decreased risk of developing cervical cancer, and, hence, would require modifications in the screening strategies targeting them; those modifications should probably include, among others, HPV genotyping instead of HPV testing, and omission of cytology triage in the case of high oncogenic HPV types positivity.<sup>24</sup> 

Fourth, the management of high-grade cervical lesions might be safely shifted to a more conservative approach in vaccinated cohorts. In those cohorts, the prevalence of the vaccine-related HPV types is lower, and high-grade cervical lesions are generally caused by non-vaccine types, which very rarely present a significant oncogenic potential and are less likely to lead to the development of cervical cancer.<sup>25</sup> This may allow for a differential approach of cervical lesions, depending on the causing HPV type, a strategy which can be useful given the adverse outcomes related to cervical treatment.<sup>26</sup> 

Fifth, in vaccinated populations, screening could be adapted through the modification of screening intervals. So, depending on the individualized cervical cancer risk of the target population, screening could be based on the assessment of various parameters affecting this risk, and not on a 1-size-fits-all approach which is the current practice in most countries.<sup>24</sup> Therefore, targeted screening, which can be more intense in populations at high-risk and less intense in low-risk populations, would be feasible and could provide a more cost-effective approach in an organized screening program. This in turn would be beneficial for public health since it would redirect budgets to health care sectors in need. 

Lastly, one of the main outcomes of RISCC concerns the utility of self-sampling in cervical cancer screening. It has already been documented that self-sampling, when used along with polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based HPV DNA screening tests, presents similar sensitivity and specificity in the detection of cervical precancer.<sup>27</sup> However, there are strict criteria in implementing selfsampling in an organized screening program pertaining to the collection device, the sample storage and transport processes, and the handling of the samples within the laboratory.<sup>28</sup> In addition, it must be noted that self-sampling works only with target-amplification-based tests, that is, PCR-based HPV DNA tests. Those tests should be initially validated for use with clinician-collected samples,<sup>29</sup> and should present similar accuracy on self-collected and physician-collected samples, following a strict protocol.<sup>30</sup> Selfsampling is a safe and effective strategy to increase cervical cancer screening participation rates, since it is acceptable to women, particularly those who do not attend regular screening. Within the RISCC sub-pilot, the acceptability of HPV self-sampling and the factors motivating women to use this method were also evaluated. The findings highlight that women’s preferences and motivations regarding self-sampling vary across different socioeconomic groups, indicating that a one-size-fits-all approach is unlikely to achieve optimal participation. To improve accessibility and uptake, screening programs should therefore offer multiple access pathways for HPV self-sampling. These may include options such as onsite self-sampling at health care or community locations, as well as the possibility of receiving self-sampling kits by mail. Providing flexible and context-sensitive approaches can help address barriers related to access, privacy, convenience and trust, thereby supporting broader participation in risk-based cervical cancer screening. 

Moreover, in order to broaden coverage, a key component is the establishment of robust systems for continuous monitoring and quality assurance. Integrated population registries, standardized quality indicators, and real-time surveillance of screening participation, vaccination uptake, and clinical outcomes are essential for evaluating program effectiveness and enabling timely policy adjustments. Continuous monitoring with the integration of linked data of screening results, HPV testing, histopathology, vaccination data, and invitation histories allows health systems to identify gaps in coverage, assess the impact of vaccination on screening performance, and optimize risk-based screening strategies as population immunity evolves.<sup>31</sup> 

## PSYCHOSOCIAL BURDEN OF HPV PRIMARY CERVICAL CANCER SCREENING

The transition to HPV-based cervical cancer screening introduces not only clinical advantages but also new psychological and communication challenges for women participating in screening programs. As HPV is a sexually transmitted infection, receiving an HPV-positive result may evoke emotional responses such as anxiety, shame, stigma, or concerns about sexual relationships. In this context, health literacy plays a critical role in how individuals understand HPV test results and screening recommendations. Many women have limited knowledge about HPV, including its high prevalence, the long latency between infection and the development of cervical cancer, and the distinction between HPV infection and disease. Without appropriate communication strategies, the introduction of HPV-based screening may unintentionally increase psychological distress, reduce trust in screening program, or negatively affect participation. Effective education is therefore essential not only to improve understanding but also to strengthen women’s motivation to participate in screening and adhere to recommended follow-up. Communication strategies should emphasize the benefits of early detection, the safety of longer screening intervals within risk-based approaches, and the importance of appropriate follow-up when HPV is detected. To ensure consistency and clarity of information, educational materials should be developed based on harmonized core messages agreed upon by key stakeholders involved in cervical cancer prevention.<sup>32,33</sup> 

## STATEMENT OF COMMITMENT

ESGO contributed to the RISCC project as a partner able to provide the perspective of an internationally recognized professional and scientific society, which has been leading the field of gynecological oncology, including the prevention of gynecological cancers, in Europe. Additionally, ENGAGe, an international consortium of patient advocacy groups in gynecological cancers, provided a patient perspective to the RISCC project, supporting patient-friendly, acceptable, and equitable interventions to modify the 1-size-fits-all cervical cancer screening, according to the proposed risk stratification. 

ESGO/ENGAGe state their commitment to the RISCC consortium; with this statement, they fulfill their role in the support, communication, and dissemination of the RISCC outcomes. They acknowledge the need for reform in cervical cancer screening in Europe, which could incorporate current and perhaps future technological advancements in the field, through the prism of patient-centered risk-based assessment. 

## RECOMMENDATIONS

ESGO/ENGAGe, following the outcomes of the RISCC project regarding risk-based cervical cancer screening, recommend the following: 

1. HPV DNA testing as a primary screening test for secondary prevention of cervical cancer. 

2. Gender-neutral vaccination to further accelerate cervical cancer elimination. 

3. Continuous bidirectional communication between health care providers and institutions who organize screening in order to define populations at high risk based on vaccination status, screening history, or participation activity. 

4. Tailored cervical cancer screening programs considering the target populations’ inherent risk of developing the disease. 

5. Modified management of cervical pre-cancer depending on the oncogenic potential. 

6. Self-sampling combined with HPV DNA testing to increase screening participation rates. 

7. Development of strategies addressing the psychological and communication aspects of HPV infection and HPV-based screening.
